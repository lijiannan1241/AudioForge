Seven Numbers I Check Before Shipping an Audiobook

Not "it sounds fine" — "all seven pass". Ten minutes before delivery saves a full redo.

2026-11-17 · 490 words

中文版 → 中文

An audiobook is expensive to redo: change one parameter and nine hours of synthesis may be gone. So delivery needs a checklist, not a listen-through.

1. Overall loudness: -19 LUFS (±0.5)

Audiobooks usually target -19 LUFS, quieter than podcast (-16) because long listening sessions in a quiet room fatigue fast. Read the output LUFS column — and watch the spread, not just the individual values. A few files at -21 means the source dynamics were never handled.

2. True peak: at or below -1 dBTP

MP3/AAC encoding overshoots. Master to -1 dBTP and the encoded file will not clip. Note this is true peak, not sample peak — they can differ by more than 1 dB, and sample peak will miss it.

3. Clipped samples: 0

Anything non-zero means the waveform was flattened at the top, which sounds like distortion and cannot be repaired. This column does not need interpretation; it needs to be zero.

4. DC offset: near zero

Offset pushes the waveform off centre, wasting headroom and producing clicks in some players. Common in older recordings and certain capture chains. The waveform view shows asymmetry immediately.

5. Chapter length and rate outliers

A 47-minute chapter followed by a 3-minute one usually means a split or OCR error, not a short chapter. Look at character count, duration and status per chapter, plus the rate-outlier rule in the TTS report. A 3-second chapter should send you back to that page's OCR result.

6. Silence ratio

Very high silence (say above 30%) implies blank pages, a table of contents, or broken paragraphs. The listener just waits for the next sentence. The TTS QA report lists it.

7. Uncovered pronunciation

Abbreviations, model numbers, names — all-caps tokens and long digit strings are the two categories most likely to be read wrong. If the dictionary does not cover them, the engine invents a reading. The report lists both; fix the glossary and re-synthesize only the affected chapters (checkpointing makes that cheap).

The delivery checklist

  • The M4B — AAC, chapter track, cover, title/author metadata;
  • Chapter audio (optional) — one file per chapter, easier to re-edit;
  • Reports — CSV and HTML, for the client and for your archive;
  • Naming — sortable, e.g. BookTitle_ch03_Title.m4b. Not "final_v2_really".

One last thing

Play chapter one in a real player, and try to skip a chapter. A broken chapter track, a missing cover, no position memory — all of these are green in the report and only the listener finds them.

Seven numbers passing does not make a good audiobook. One of them failing guarantees someone will hear it.
