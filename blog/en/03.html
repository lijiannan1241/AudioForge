Choosing One Voice Out of 318 — Stop Trusting "That Sounds Good"

Picking a narrator is the most expensive decision in an audiobook — changing your mind means redoing nine hours. Make the choice comparable instead of gut-feel.

2026-11-17 · 517 words

中文版 → 中文

Volume lies to you

Play the same paragraph in two voices, and make A three decibels louder. Almost everyone picks A, and says it sounds "clearer, more present". That is not taste; that is how hearing works.

So any side-by-side comparison is invalid: the difference you hear includes a level difference. The only fair method is to match loudness first, then switch in place.

How to run one blind test

Pick three representative passages. Narrative (timbre and baseline pace), dialogue (breath, stress, emotion), and a jargon-dense passage (are abbreviations and proper nouns read correctly). Twenty to forty seconds each — longer and your ears fatigue.

Fix the target. Audiobooks usually sit at -19 LUFS (podcasts -16, game VO -23). Every candidate must be matched to that target first, or you are back to "louder wins".

Switch at the same position, not play-each-once. The point is the same sentence, the same instant, back and forth. After three passes you stop hearing "niceness" and start hearing what matters: who stresses mechanically, who drops the end of a sentence, who reads a comma as a full stop.

Write the numbers down. Duration, LUFS, true peak. They do not replace your ears, but when two voices tie, the one with steadier pace and more peak headroom is the safer bet.

Doing it in LoudPipe

A counterintuitive tip

Pick two, not one. Choose a primary and a backup, then re-listen after the first three chapters. Some voices shine on short lines and turn monotone over long paragraphs; some start flat and settle in. Swapping after three chapters costs far less than redoing nine hours.

If the book is dialogue-heavy, add multi-role casting: regex routes different characters' lines to different voices and rates, and the narrator handles only prose. One voice for nine hours and "narrator plus three characters" produce very different fatigue curves.

Make it a process

Choosing a narrator should not be "open the preview, click the one that feels right". It should be: three passages → matched loudness → in-place blind test → record the numbers → pick two → recheck after three chapters.

Fifteen minutes, against a nine-hour redo.
